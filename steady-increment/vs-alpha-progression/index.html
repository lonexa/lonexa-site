An Alpha Progression alternative you buy once

Prices checked 5 September 2026.

Alpha Progression is a good app. This one was built by somebody who paid for it, used it for sixteen months, and then wrote an importer for its export format — so the comparison below is specific rather than dismissive.

The short version

Alpha Progression charges $79.99 a year, every year, for a deterministic progression engine. Steady Increment charges $59.99 once for one that also models the equipment in front of you, and it imports your Alpha Progression history so you keep every set you have already logged.

What Alpha Progression does well, and honestly

It is a real engine, not a logger with a graph. It generates plans, it progresses them, it has a large exercise library, and it has been shipping long enough to be dependable. Its 10RM figures are consistent and its export is complete. If you are happy paying yearly for it, it is not a bad deal — it is a good app at a subscription price.

It also has things this app does not: a longer track record, a much larger user base, and years of polish. This is a new app. That is a real difference and it is worth weighing.

Where this app is different

1. It knows what your gym can physically load

This is the thing that made the app exist. A progression engine that says “187.5 lb” to somebody standing at a machine whose stack moves in tens has not finished the job. Steady Increment models the bar, the plates the gym actually stocks, and each machine’s own smallest jump — which it infers from your logged weights rather than asking you to configure it.

On a rack holding only 45s and 25s, asked for 200, it will tell you 195, because that is reachable and 200 is not. Machines that stop at 275 lb never get asked for 280. Drop sets drop to a pin that exists.

2. It names the fix when a lift stalls

Both apps notice a stall. This one says which of three things to do about it — deload, change the rep range, or swap the movement — rather than leaving you to work out what the flag means.

3. Machine cardio is a first-class citizen

Treadmill, bike, rower, stair and elliptical each get their own progression rules, their own interval structures and their own console units. Duration and intensity are advanced in alternation rather than together, deliberately, because pushing both at once is how people wreck an aerobic base.

4. The coach is included, and it cannot invent a number

Every number comes from the engine. The coach reads them and tells you what it makes of the session. There is no field in which a language model could return a weight, so a recommendation is always reproducible. Upgrading that coach to a language model is $4.99 a month and entirely optional.

5. You stop paying

Two years of Alpha Progression is $159.98. Three is $239.97, and it keeps going. $59.99 is $59.99.

Your history comes with you, and it checks itself

Export your workouts from Alpha Progression and open the file here. Sixteen months — 2,846 sets across 87 distinct exercises — imports with nothing dropped and every exercise matched to the library without asking.

There is a nice property in this one. Alpha Progression’s displayed 10RM is raw Epley: weight × (1 + reps/30) × 0.75. Because the export carries those figures, an imported set can be checked against the number Alpha Progression itself reported for it. If the parser ever mis-read a row, the arithmetic would disagree. The import verifies itself against the app it came from.

Exercises are matched on name and equipment, which matters more than it sounds: an Alpha Progression export uses “Lateral Raises” for three different exercises — machine, dumbbell and band. Merging them would put 145 lb machine sets and 20 lb dumbbell sets on one strength curve and quietly ruin both.

Who should not switch

If you are on iPhone, this app does not exist for you yet. If you need a social feed or a watch app, it does not have either. And if Alpha Progression is working for you and the yearly price does not bother you, there is no reason to move — you would be trading a mature app for a new one to gain equipment modelling and a one-time price.

The case for switching is narrow and specific: you want the engine to account for the equipment you actually train on, and you would rather pay once.
